## Core principle

AGI should not be defined as a larger pattern predictor alone. A human-like general system would need **inductive structure, grounded models, active learning, flexible reasoning, and self-monitoring**. The human brain appears to begin with evolved predispositions rather than fully formed adult concepts; therefore, the design target is an effective **developmental starting kit**, not a complete set of innate facts. [2, 4, 14, 17]

## 1. Innate or built-in representational primitives

- [ ] **Object representation:** Track objects as persistent entities with boundaries, continuity, cohesion, and approximate physical behavior.
- [ ] **Agent representation:** Distinguish agents from inert objects and represent goals, actions, attention, and intentions.
- [ ] **Spatial representation:** Maintain geometric and topological maps for navigation and scene understanding.
- [ ] **Numerical representation:** Support approximate magnitude and small-number tracking before relying on language or symbols.
- [ ] **Temporal representation:** Model order, duration, change, and event persistence.
- [ ] **Self/world distinction:** Separate the system’s own body, actions, observations, and knowledge from external entities.

These should function as priors or architectural biases, not as rigid rules. Related object, agent, spatial, and numerical abilities emerge early in human development and have analogues in other animals. [4, 5, 17, 18] The replication record for some early-capacity claims is uneven [32], and the innate number-sense claim in particular is contested [33]. Since 2023, generic networks trained on child-eye data have acquired visual object categories [34] and word–referent mappings [35] without explicit object or agent priors, keeping the case for hard-wired primitives empirically open.

## 2. Learning and abstraction

- [ ] **Statistical learning:** Extract regularities from sequences, sensory inputs, language, and social interaction.
- [ ] **Category formation:** Form reusable categories from sparse and varied examples.
- [ ] **Overhypothesis learning:** Infer abstract rules about which kinds of patterns or categories are likely to exist.
- [ ] **Relational abstraction:** Represent relations such as same/different, cause/effect, part/whole, before/after, and greater/less.
- [ ] **Compositionality:** Combine known parts and relations to construct genuinely novel concepts and plans.
- [ ] **Analogy and transfer:** Map relational structure from a familiar domain to a new domain.
- [ ] **Variable binding:** Preserve the roles of entities in a relation rather than merely matching their surface features.

**Design interpretation:** abstraction is plausibly innate as a set of biases and proto-conceptual mechanisms, but mature symbolic abstraction is developed through experience, language, and culture. The checklist should therefore require both preconfigured structure and learning-driven refinement. [9, 13, 26, 28, 30] Meta-learned transformers have achieved systematic generalization without built-in symbolic machinery [36], so preconfigured structure remains a well-motivated hypothesis rather than a closed question.

## 3. Grounding and world modeling

- [ ] **Multimodal grounding:** Connect words, symbols, images, sounds, actions, and consequences to shared entities and situations.
- [ ] **Causal models:** Distinguish correlation from intervention, mechanism, and counterfactual possibility. [43]
- [ ] **Everyday physics:** Predict what objects will do when moved, blocked, supported, broken, or combined.
- [ ] **Psychological models:** Represent what other agents know, want, believe, perceive, and may do next.
- [ ] **World-model updating:** Revise beliefs when observations conflict with predictions.
- [ ] **Counterfactual simulation:** Ask what would happen if an action, event, or assumption were changed. [43]

**Acceptance test:** the system should solve novel problems after a small number of grounded demonstrations, not only after exposure to many linguistic descriptions. [31]

## 4. Agency, exploration, and action

- [ ] **Embodied or action-grounded interaction:** Learn through acting, observing consequences, and correcting errors. [42]
- [ ] **Active information seeking:** Choose experiments, questions, or observations that reduce uncertainty.
- [ ] **Intrinsic motivation:** Explore unfamiliar situations without requiring a complete externally supplied reward function. [40, 41]
- [ ] **Hierarchical planning:** Decompose long-term goals into subgoals and executable actions.
- [ ] **Online adaptation:** Learn continuously without catastrophically overwriting earlier competencies. [37, 38]
- [ ] **Resource awareness:** Account for time, energy, memory, uncertainty, and computational limits.

A system that only generates plausible descriptions but cannot test, revise, and operationalize its models should not be considered fully general.

## 5. Memory and learning across time

- [ ] **Working memory:** Maintain relevant entities, goals, constraints, and intermediate results during reasoning.
- [ ] **Episodic memory:** Store specific experiences with context, source, time, and consequences.
- [ ] **Semantic memory:** Consolidate general knowledge extracted from many episodes.
- [ ] **Procedural memory:** Retain skills and action policies.
- [ ] **Memory retrieval by meaning:** Retrieve experiences by situation, relation, goal, and causal relevance—not only by token similarity.
- [ ] **Consolidation and forgetting:** Compress, reorganize, and discard information while preserving useful structure.
- [ ] **Provenance tracking:** Record whether a belief came from observation, inference, instruction, simulation, or another agent.

Complementary learning systems theory motivates separate fast episodic and slow semantic learning pathways with offline consolidation between them. [39]

## 6. Social and cultural intelligence

- [ ] **Joint attention:** Coordinate attention with another agent toward a shared object or goal.
- [ ] **Instruction following and teaching:** Learn efficiently from demonstrations, explanations, correction, and pedagogy.
- [ ] **Shared intentionality:** Represent cooperative goals and norms that are not reducible to individual rewards.
- [ ] **Pragmatic language understanding:** Infer what a speaker means in context, including omissions and indirect requests.
- [ ] **Norm and value learning:** Identify social expectations while distinguishing description from endorsement.
- [ ] **Perspective taking:** Model how different agents may hold different information or beliefs.
- [ ] **Cultural accumulation:** Preserve, improve, and transmit knowledge across generations or system versions.

Human general intelligence is amplified by social learning and cumulative culture, so social cognition may be a capability multiplier rather than an optional interface feature. [1, 6, 7, 21, 22]

## 7. Metacognition and reliability

- [ ] **Uncertainty estimation:** Distinguish confidence from correctness.
- [ ] **Error monitoring:** Detect contradictions, failed plans, hallucinations, and model mismatch.
- [ ] **Self-modeling:** Represent the system’s own capabilities, limitations, tools, memories, and current state.
- [ ] **Deliberation control:** Decide when to answer quickly, retrieve information, run a simulation, ask a question, or defer.
- [ ] **Reflective revision:** Inspect and revise reasoning rather than merely produce a second fluent answer.
- [ ] **Goal and value stability:** Detect conflicts between local incentives and higher-level objectives.
- [ ] **Safe social behavior:** Anticipate effects on people, institutions, and shared environments.

Early metacognitive capacities may provide developmental foundations, but explicit self-reflection is substantially elaborated through development and language. [15]

## 8. Evaluation criteria for “true” generality

An AGI candidate should be tested on whether it can:

- [ ] Generalize from sparse data to structurally new cases.
- [ ] Transfer a relation or skill across different modalities and domains.
- [ ] Learn from interaction rather than only from static datasets.
- [ ] Explain and use causal structure under interventions.
- [ ] Plan over long horizons with changing conditions.
- [ ] Learn continuously without losing prior capabilities.
- [ ] Distinguish familiar surface patterns from deeper relational similarity.
- [ ] Ask useful questions when information is insufficient.
- [ ] Know when it is uncertain or wrong.
- [ ] Coordinate with humans whose goals, beliefs, and knowledge differ.
- [ ] Develop new abstractions that were not explicitly listed in training data.

Relevant evaluation frameworks: intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency [44], with ARC-style tasks as a concrete benchmark [45]; a performance × generality × autonomy taxonomy [46].

## Bottom line

The strongest design lesson is not “copy every human cognitive module.” It is: **build the right inductive biases, then let them develop through grounded interaction, social learning, and reflection.** Pattern matching is likely one component of intelligence; AGI would additionally require structured representations, causal and counterfactual modeling, compositional abstraction, memory, agency, social understanding, and metacognitive control. The exact combination remains an empirical research question rather than a settled blueprint. [2, 9, 13, 14]
