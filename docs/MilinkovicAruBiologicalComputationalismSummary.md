## The Problem and the Stalemate

The paper opens by diagnosing a stalemate in consciousness studies between two entrenched camps:

- **Computational functionalism**: consciousness is fully determined by the right pattern of information processing, independent of physical substrate.
- **Biological naturalism**: consciousness is inseparable from the concrete properties of living brains and bodies.

The authors argue neither position alone is adequate, and the debate has stagnated because there is no coherent framework explaining *how* biological computation actually differs from digital computation, or why that difference matters for consciousness. This paper proposes to fill that gap.

## The Thesis: Biological Computationalism

The core claim is that **the substrate is the algorithm** — in biological systems, computation cannot be abstracted away from its physical realization. This is not a "biology-or-nothing" mysticism: the authors explicitly leave open the possibility that consciousness could occur in non-carbon substrates, provided those substrates instantiate the *right class* of computation. The key question shifts from "Is it biological?" to "Does it compute in the right way?"

## Three Defining Features of Biological Computation

### 1. Hybrid computation (discrete + continuous)
Neurons spike — discrete, digital-like events — but this spiking is embedded in an ocean of continuous dynamics: graded membrane potentials, ion flows, extracellular field potentials, chemical diffusion, and ephaptic (field-based, non-synaptic) coupling between cells. These aren't background noise; they are information-carrying. Spikes "ride atop" continuous processes, which themselves ride atop neuroanatomy. The system is neither digital nor analog: it is irreducibly hybrid. Dendritic computation is a prime example — dendrites perform non-linear local operations, temporally coarse-graining inputs before they ever reach the cell body to trigger a spike.

### 2. Scale-inseparability
In engineered digital systems, abstraction layers are clean and unidirectional: a high-level programming language does not alter transistor structure in real time. In the brain, there is no such clean hardware/software divide. Causal influence flows *bidirectionally across scales* — from ion channels to dendrites to circuits to whole-brain oscillations and back down. The authors coin the term **heterarchy** (vs. hierarchy) for this structure, drawing an analogy to Tarski hierarchies in formal logic: proving statements at one level requires simultaneous access to other levels, with continuous co-determination rather than unidirectional queries. Consciousness, on this view, may require a form of emergent closure across scales that digital simulations — which model scales as separate mathematical objects — cannot natively replicate.

A specific molecular example given: Protein Kinase A (PKA) acts as a continuous evidence-accumulation mechanism at the subcellular level, comparing local activity with postsynaptic dynamics and determining when a network-level transition is warranted. This is subcellular computation directly triggering network-level discrete state changes — scales co-determining each other.

### 3. Metabolic grounding
The brain runs on ~20 watts. This is not a footnote — it is constitutive. Unlike AI systems that can simply add more compute, the brain must achieve intelligence under strict energy scarcity. This forces specific organizational strategies:
- **Coarse-graining**: aggregating microscopic discrete events into macroscopic continuous dynamics to save energy (e.g., non-spiking graded potentials carry more information per joule than spiking).
- **Cross-scale integration**: multiscale interdependencies are not accidental complexity but an energy-optimization strategy.
- **Heterarchical topology**: the result is a structure fundamentally different from engineered hierarchies, where changing implementation changes computation because the two are entangled.

The authors call metabolic constraints "constitutive forces" that shape how neural systems process, transmit, and integrate information across scales.

## Why Current AI Cannot Be Conscious

The paper directly addresses LLMs and deep learning. Current AI systems are built on **von Neumann architectures** with strict separation between memory, processing, and control, and strict separation between software (algorithm) and hardware (implementation). They **simulate functions** — approximating input-output mappings — but their computation is a digital procedure running on hardware designed for an entirely different computational style.

The authors argue the absence of consciousness in such systems is not a matter of insufficient scale, data, or architectural complexity. It reflects a deeper structural divide: these systems lack hybrid processing, scale-inseparable dynamics, and metabolic (energetic) grounding. They simulate what consciousness does without instantiating how it works physically.

## What Would Artificial Consciousness Require?

The paper does *not* say artificial consciousness is impossible. Instead, it redirects the engineering question. Synthetic minds may require:

- **Biomimetic substrates** that natively support hybrid, multiscale processing.
- **Structured stochasticity** and history-dependent memory analogous to biological synapses.
- Systems where energy, structure, and dynamics are integrated rather than separated — where the computing is inseparable from the physical dynamics of the machine.

The authors reframe the challenge: the question is not "What algorithm should we run?" but "**What kind of physical system must exist for the algorithm to be inseparable from its own dynamics?**"

## Positioning in the Literature

The paper is a theoretical/philosophical contribution (classified as a literature review by the journal). It sits in the tradition of critics of strong computational functionalism (e.g., Searle's biological naturalism), but stakes a more specific and technically grounded position. It cites the neuroscience of dendritic computation, ephaptic coupling, oscillatory dynamics, and metabolic neuroscience to ground what might otherwise be a philosophical claim.
